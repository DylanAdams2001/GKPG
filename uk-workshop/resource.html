What Supported Housing Actually Is

The structural difference between this and an ordinary buy to let.

In an ordinary buy to let, your income depends on a tenant. You find one, you hope they pay, and when they leave you carry the void until the next one arrives. Your yield is whatever survives that process.

Supported housing works the other way round. The property is let to a housing provider on a long term contracted agreement. The provider houses the people who need supported accommodation and takes on the tenant relationship entirely. Your agreement is with the provider, not with the occupant.

That single change is where the rest of the model comes from. Because the rent is contracted rather than market-dependent, it does not fall when the local rental market softens, and it does not stop when an occupant moves out. The provider carries that risk, which is what allows the income to be described as assured rather than projected.

The distinction worth holding on to: this is not a bet on a location. An ordinary buy to let asks you to pick the right town and the right street at the right time. This model asks you to hold an asset with a contracted income attached to it. Those two things fail in completely different ways, and for completely different reasons.

02  ·  The Numbers

Where The 16.8% Comes From

A net figure, calculated after costs, not a headline gross number.

Most yields quoted in property marketing are gross. They describe the rent before anything is taken out of it, which makes them close to meaningless as a comparison, because the costs that follow vary enormously between models.

The 16.8% is a net figure. It is what remains after the costs of running the property have already been accounted for, which is why it can be compared directly against the 4 to 5% net that a typical residential buy to let returns.

Illustrative worked example. A three bedroom semi-detached at a £325,000 purchase price, on a 16.8% net yield, produces £54,600 a year. The workshop quoted £53,000, which is the same calculation rounded down. This is an illustration of the arithmetic, not an offer, a quotation, or a projection for any specific property.

Why the number can be this much higher

Three things account for most of the gap between this and a standard buy to let:

What changes, against an ordinary buy to let
No voids
Rent is contracted with the provider, so it continues between occupants
No letting or renewal fees
No agent re-letting the property every twelve months
Maintenance within the agreement
Day to day upkeep sits with the provider rather than with you
Net yield to you 16.8%
Compared with what you may hold now. If your existing rentals return 4 to 5% net, the difference is not a better location or a harder-working agent. It is that the three lines above have been removed from the model rather than minimised within it.

What 25 Years Actually Covers

The question worth asking of any assurance: what happens on the day it's tested.

A long agreement is only as good as its behaviour under stress. The useful question is not how many years are on the front page, but what happens the first time something goes wrong.

Covered by the agreement

  • Rent continues if the property is unoccupied between placements
  • Rent continues if an occupant stops paying, that is the provider's exposure, not yours
  • Day to day maintenance and repairs within the agreed scope
  • Tenant sourcing, placement and all occupant contact
  • Rent collection and payment to you on a fixed cycle
On the word "assured". Assured means the rent is contracted for the term rather than dependent on occupancy or on market conditions. It does not mean risk has been removed from the investment. It has been moved, from you to the provider, and it is worth understanding the provider's own standing before you rely on that. We will go through exactly who the agreement is with on a call.
Worth reading before you commit: the agreement itself. Not a summary of it, the document. We will send it in full, and we would encourage you to have a solicitor read it. Any model that cannot survive that scrutiny is not one you should be in.

04  ·  Day To Day

What "Hands‑Off" Means In Practice

Specifically, what lands in your inbox after completion, and what doesn't.

"Fully managed" is used loosely across the industry, and often means little more than an agent who forwards you problems rather than solving them. It is worth being precise about it.

You will never deal with

  • Finding or vetting occupants
  • Chasing rent arrears
  • Maintenance calls, at any hour
  • Inspections and check‑ins
  • Deposit disputes
  • Re‑letting the property between occupants

You will receive

  • Rent on a fixed payment cycle
  • A monthly statement
  • A named point of contact
  • Notice of anything requiring an owner decision
This works the same from anywhere. Because there is no operational involvement expected of you, holding one of these from another country is no different from holding one from the next town. Our existing investors are spread across the UK, Hong Kong and Singapore, and the process is identical for all of them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a mortgage, or does this have to be cash?

Either. You can buy outright or use a buy to let mortgage. Finance is available subject to status and lender approval, and we can point you at lenders who already understand this structure.

Is the 16.8% really net?

Yes. It is calculated after the costs of running the property, not before. That is what makes it comparable to the 4 to 5% net that a standard buy to let typically returns, rather than to a gross headline figure.

What happens if the occupant stops paying?

Your agreement is with the housing provider, not the occupant, so non‑payment by an occupant is the provider's exposure rather than yours. Your rent continues under the terms of the agreement.

What happens if the provider fails?

You own the freehold title outright, so the asset remains yours in any circumstance. The contracted income depends on the provider, which is why their standing is a fair thing to ask about, and we will go through it openly on a call.

Do I own the property, or is this a fund or a scheme?

You own it. Freehold title, registered in your name, the same as any other property purchase. This is not a fund, a share, or a collective investment.

Can I sell it later?

Yes, it is your asset to sell. Bear in mind that property is generally illiquid and a sale takes time, and that a property with an agreement attached appeals to a particular kind of buyer. Worth discussing against your own timescale before you commit.

Can I buy this through a limited company?

Yes, and we would encourage it. Most investors hold and operate these through a company structure. How that affects your own tax position depends entirely on your circumstances, so it is worth putting to your accountant before you decide either way.

Is any of this financial advice?

No. This guide is general information only. It is not personal financial advice and nothing here is a recommendation. We would always encourage independent financial and legal advice before committing any capital.
